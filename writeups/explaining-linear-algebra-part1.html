Explaining Linear Algebra (Part 1)

Preface

You’ve used linear regression before. You want to know why it works.

For now, this is a personal resource for what linear algebra is. Later this becomes the mathematical basis for linear regression.

1. How we’re taught “math”

Let’s start by clearing our minds almost completely. When you close your eyes, you should picture a vast field of wheat. Wind is blowing, and brushing the grains against one another. For a second, we forget everything you know.

Now open your eyes, and answer this: What is math?

Personally, several things come to mind. Here are some things I hear about math all the time.

  • “Math got hard once it started having letters”
  • “When will I use PEMDAS in real life?”
  • “If we have calculators, why do we need to learn math?”

And these are all pretty fair points. Even if we don’t call it math per se, we use it every day. We count things. That’s math. We add and subtract things in our mind. For example, how late is the bus? If it was scheduled to come at 10:15 pm and it’s currently 10:17, then it’s two minutes past due. That’s math.

So it’s no wonder people get tired of it once we start adding letters. It gets more difficult, but it’s kind of… pointless. At least compared to the math we learned before. Like dividing. And adding. And counting. Adding \(x\) into the mix just seems like pointless torture.

And that’s partially right. Math really has no purpose.

And it keeps getting harder. We learn basic math in elementary school. That’s just called “math”. Then we move on to middle school, and get tasked with Algebra. Then we move on to high school, and get tasked with Geometry. And then we move on again, to Algebra 2. I’m going to lay it out here:

  • Arithmetic (Elementary school math)
  • Algebra I
  • Geometry
  • Algebra II
  • Pre-Calculus
  • Calculus

And just beyond that…

  • Statistics
  • Linear Algebra
  • Real Analysis
  • etc.

We just keep going. But why? Well just like anything else in life, there is zero purpose to doing math. Just like there’s zero purpose to learning about history. Or art. Or language. Or biology. Or anything at all.

But unlike those other things, like history, art, language or biology, math is uniquely complicated. Let me explain why.

2. So what is math, really?

If you want to learn things that are more advanced in history, you learn more details about the historical events. You read about other countries. You read more. In the fine arts, you study more painters. You try to learn all of the artistic movememnts. Maybe you try to create some of your own art, like painting, drawing or sculpting. If you wanted to learn advanced language, you would understand linguistics. You would understand the surface and deep-structure of composing sentences. You would learn another language. If you wanted to learn more about biology, you would learn as much as you can about how your own body works. You would learn about the extent to which we understand life and the physical processes which sustain living organisms.

History, art, language and biology are subjects. And knowing more means something very obvious: you try to understand more of what is. All of those subjects are observations on the physical world. You study what the world is. And you learn as much of what is as you can.

You may have never thought of this before, but the subject of math is rules. Nobody ever calls it that. And I was too scared to ask for a long time. People say that math feels useless. It feels like a lot of memorization. And it partially is. Because math is about the rules we can impose on things, specifically.

So for a second, let’s recall the typical progression of how we learn math in school:

  • Arithmetic (Elementary school math)
  • Algebra I
  • Geometry
  • Algebra II
  • Pre-Calculus
  • Calculus

And let’s DESTROY IT.

[Image of destroying a wall.]

Math is about things and rules.

And it always has been. All of these sub-subjects in math, Arithmetic, Algebra, Geometry, etc. They’re all about introducing new things and new rules. Or different ways we can apply those rules to new things.

For example, math in elementary school. In the beginning, math starts with defining basic things: numbers. And two rules: addition and subtraction. Let’s play around with them.

\[2 + 2 = 4\] \[4 + 8 = 12\] \[100 + 100 = 200\] \[40,0000 - 10 = 39,990\]

Alright, we’re done here.

But then say you have a really good idea of numbers and how addition and subtraction work. Do you think you could handle two new rules? In come division and multiplication. On the exact same numbers.

\[6 \times 2 = 12\] \[44 \times 3 = 132\] \[15 \div 5 = 3\] \[7 \div 2 = 3.5\]

Woah. That’s something new to us, the number \(3.5\). It’s a new thing. That’s why along with multiplication and division, we learn about decimals. Floating point numbers.

That’s basically all of the math you will do in elementary school.

But math continues being the same. You keep adding new rules and new things.

In Algebra I you learn about something called a “variable”, conventionally written as \(x\). It behaves just like a number. But you can’t exactly compute with it. Because we don’t know what it is. But we DO know that it’s a number. And so we treat it as exactly that, a number we don’t know yet.

Look here:

\[3x = 12\]

We know that \(x\) is a number. And so it makes sense that if it’s a number, we could multiply another number. Like \(3\).

3. Defining what an “algebra” is

What we’ve just demonstrated here is . Generally when we create rules, we call that an “algebra”. An algebra is a ruleset.

4. Defining what “linear algebra”

Linear algebra is one of the many algebras.

There are many different kinds of algebras. Like Boolean algebra. Relational algebra. Tensor algebra.

We’re about to talk about something called a “vector space”. A vector space is a mathematical criteria that qualifies a basic set of things called “vectors”. Linear algebra is specifically the algebra of vectors. It might as well be called “vectora algebra”.
