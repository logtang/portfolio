1. Linear Regression

First, a refresher. Let’s make sure we’re on the same page about what linear regression is doing in the first place.

1.1 What is linear regression?

This writeup is called “Explaining Linear Regression”, but what even is linear regresssion?

First of all, let’s define machine learning: which is loosely speaking, using math and optimization to create an equation by “training” on a dataset. In practice, this means that we can generalize to unseen data. And therefore, predict it.

1.2 When can we perform a linear regression?

Linear regression works on a specific type of data. Although it may seem generic, when we say “data” here, what we’re talking about is actually a specific kind of data called “tabular data”

So let’s define tabular data, which to do that we need to define what a single tabular observation is.

A single tabular observation comes as a tuple. That’s something like \((t_1, t_2, t_3, ..., t_n)\) where the tuple is of length \(n\). There are n-many attributes. So tabular data is just a bunch of these observations, each their own tuple. Of the same length. In an arbitrary order.

So tabular data comes in this form:

\[ R = \left\{ \begin{array}{c} (t_{1,1},\ t_{1,2},\ \dots,\ t_{1,n}), \\ (t_{2,1},\ t_{2,2},\ \dots,\ t_{2,n}), \\ \vdots \\ (t_{m,1},\ t_{m,2},\ \dots,\ t_{m,n}) \end{array} \right\} \]

Simply put, tabular data comes as a table with \(m\)-many rows and \(n\)-many features.

1.3 How the model works

For reference, here’s the model we’re talking about when we say “linear regression”. Here’s a quick example of how we use it.

\[\hat{y_i} = \beta_nx_n + ... + \beta_1x_1 + \beta_0 + e_i\]

1.3.4 Identify a target variable

If we want to perform a linear regression, we need to choose a “target variable” to predict. For this dataset of houses, we want to predict the price of the house.

How we can visualize that is that we’re effectively ripping the entire column out of our dataset, and setting it to the side. We can visualize that in what’s called an “augmented matrix”.

\[ \left[ \begin{array}{ccccc|c} 1450 & 3 & 2 & 1998 & 6000 & 320{,}000 \\ 2100 & 4 & 3 & 2012 & 8500 & 465{,}000 \\ 980 & 2 & 1 & 1965 & 3200 & 215{,}000 \end{array} \right] \]

1.3.5 Fit the model

Remember, originally there were 6 total features. After ripping one of the features, we’re left with 5 and a target variable, price. So effectively, we have committed to this model.

\[ \epsilon_1 + \beta_0 + \beta_1(x_1) + \beta_2(x_2) + \beta_3(x_3) + \beta_4(x_4) + \beta_5(x_5) = \hat{y_1}\\ \epsilon_2 + \beta_0 + \beta_1(x_1) + \beta_2(x_2) + \beta_3(x_3) + \beta_4(x_4) + \beta_5(x_5) = \hat{y_2}\\ \epsilon_3 + \beta_0 + \beta_1(x_1) + \beta_2(x_2) + \beta_3(x_3) + \beta_4(x_4) + \beta_5(x_5) = \hat{y_3} \]

Finding the linear regression model is then just finding the weights, each of the \(\beta_i\) which give the “best” predictions across every weighted sum simultaneously.

2. Solving Systems of Equations

Ultimately, what we want to do is solve for the weights. You’ve seen something like this before:

\[3x = 12\]

I mean, this is just basic high school algebra. Where the variable \(x\) is some unknown number. We solve for the unknown by rearranging this equation such that we can isolate the unknown in terms of knowns.

\[x = \frac{12}{3} = 4\]

And that’s what we’re going to try do with the weights. We want to know the values of the weights. And we’re going to use algebra to do that!

So we know how to solve a simple algebraic equation like \(3x = 12\). So hypothetically, if \(x\) was the weights, we could solve for that to! Something like:

\[3(weights) = 12\]

Except there’s an issue here: \(x\) is a single number. That’s why we’re able to solve for it.

\[x = \text{a number}\]

But if we want to solve for the weights of a linear regression, that come in this form:

\[\beta = (\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3, \beta_4, \beta_5)\]

2.1 Refresher of systems of equations

Let’s go over a brief refresher on the vocabulary of systems of equations.

First of all, an equation is a mathematical statement, showing that two things are equal. That’s what the “equals” sign means, \(=\)! And that’s why it looks like that. The two bars of the equals sign are the same as each other. Just like how both sides of the equals sign are always supposed to be the same. No matter how they’re expressed.

So look at this single equation: \(3x = 12\)

This is the equation we looked at earlier. It’s a single equation. There’s just one statement. And there’s a single unknown, the variable \(x\)

So a system of equations: is more than one equation (multiple mathematical statements) which all share variables.

e.g. 

\[ \begin{aligned} x + y &= 5 \\ x - y &= 1 \end{aligned} \]

This is also a system of equations

\[ \begin{aligned} v + w + x + y + z &= 15 \\ 2v - w + x - y + z &= 4 \\ v + 2w - x + y - z &= 1 \\ 3v + w + 2x - 2y + z &= 8 \\ v - w - x + 2y + 3z &= 19 \end{aligned} \]

How we “solve” a system of equations is just like how we solve a single equation. We try to find all of the values we could put in place of the unknowns which would make all of the equations true at once!

For a single equation, \(3x = 12\) here the solution is \({4}\). That’s the only value which makes the equation true.

\[3(4) = 12\]

This is a true statement. The value which makes this statement true is the solution!

Any other number, like \(5\) for example, I mean you could technically still put them in place of the unknown

\[3(5) = 12\]

But that makes the statement false instead.

But how about we try a different example.

e.g. 

\[x^2 + 3 = 7\]

What’s the solution to this equation?

Let’s try and isolate the unknown \(x\). First, let’s get rid of the three by subtracting it from both sides.

\[x^2 = 4\]

Now we

So our solution the solution (unique values that solve our equation) are \(2\) and \(-2\). If someone asks us what the solution is, we would present them this: \({-2, 2}\).

Earlier you might have noticed that we put the solution \(4\) in brackets. We wrote it as \({4}\). And that’s because technically the formal name for the values which solve the equation is the solution set. There can be more than one value for the unknown that makes the statement true!

That’s why we need a set. A set is a collection, which can help us contain more than one value.

We’ve just solved two individual equations. But how about an entire system of equations?

2.2 Solving systems of linear equations (Gauss-Jordan elimination)

Throughout the linear algebra series we alluded to ways we could solve for systems of linear equations. Now let’s learn how that’s done.

First, look here:

\[ \begin{aligned} x + 2y + 3z &= 14 \\ 2x + 5y + 6z &= 30 \\ -x + 2y + 3z &= 12 \end{aligned} \]

We have a system of linear equations. In school, we learn to solve systems of equations with multiple different tricks. There’s substitution, subtraction and equating. You don’t need to remember how to do those. Generally they’re only useful for if we have just two equations.

But oftentimes we have more than two equations. Even just three equations makes it take a lot longer. The traditional high school methods suck, honestly. Just trust me. They take a long time. Like, a really really long time.

But lucky for us, there’s a general method that’s easier than the high school methods! It’s called Gauss-Jordan elimination. Here’s how it works.

Like earlier, we’re going to fit this into an “augmented matrix” which is just when we use a bar to separate \[ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 3 & 14 \\ 2 & 5 & 6 & 30 \\ -1 & 2 & 3 & 12 \end{array}\right] \]

The augmented matrix stuff is all just aesthetics. We didn’t change anything. At all. But what it lets us do is it helps us keep track of how we’re really going to solve this system of linear equations. We’re going to use something called “Gaussian-Jordan elimination”. To be honest, it’s a bit tedious. I’ll come back and explain this in great detail.

But simply put, we’re trying to achieve something called the “identity matrix”, which is when

We achieve that with row operations in two phases:

The first is called the “forward phase” where we establish REF, then RREF. The second is called the “backward phase” where we

\[ \xrightarrow{R_2 \,\leftarrow\, R_2 - 2R_1} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 3 & 14 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 \\ -1 & 2 & 3 & 12 \end{array}\right] \]

\[ \xrightarrow{R_3 \,\leftarrow\, R_3 + R_1} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 3 & 14 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 4 & 6 & 26 \end{array}\right] \]

\[ \xrightarrow{R_3 \,\leftarrow\, R_3 - 4R_2} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 3 & 14 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 & 18 \end{array}\right] \]

\[ \xrightarrow{R_3 \,\leftarrow\, \frac{1}{6}R_3} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 3 & 14 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \end{array}\right] \]

End of the forward phase

\[ \xrightarrow{R_1 \,\leftarrow\, R_1 - 3R_3} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 0 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \end{array}\right] \]

\[ \xrightarrow{R_1 \,\leftarrow\, R_1 - 2R_2} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \end{array}\right] \]

End of the backward phase. We’ve achieved the identity matrix! And we can immediately conclude that:

\[ x = 1, \quad y = 2, \quad z = 3 \]

We can verify our results here.

\[ \begin{aligned} (1) + 2(2) + 3(3) &= 14 \\ 2(1) + 5(2) + 6(3) &= 30 \\ -(1) + 2(2) + 3(3) &= 12 \end{aligned} \]

We solved the system of equations!

The solution set is given by \({(x=1, y=2, z=3)}\)

But this is where it gets complicated. This was meant to be a simple example, just like how we solved \(3x=12\) earlier.

We got lucky here. There was just one solution. That’s true for when the count of constraints = the count of unknowns (\(\#\text{ of constraints} = \#\text{ of unknowns}\)).

But when happens when \(\#\text{ of constraints} \neq \#\text{ of unknowns}\)?

  • When \(\#\text{ of constraints} > \#\text{ of unknowns}\): There are no solutions

The length of the solution set is zero (i.e. \(|\text{solution set}| = 0\))

  • When \(\#\text{ of constraints} < \#\text{ of unknowns}\): There are infinitely many solutions

The length of the solution set is zero (i.e. \(|\text{solution set}| = \infty\))

So basically, the point of this is to say that we can always “solve” a system of linear equations by answering what the solution set is. But in order to find a single solution, the system has to meet a certain criteria to begin with. The count of constraints must equal the count of unknowns. That’s called a square system.

2.3 Why we can’t solve for the slope coefficients like a normal system of equations

Maybe you’ve noticed already. But the problem of linear regression is essentially trying to find a single solution to a non-square system of linear equations.

And we know that’s impossible. There is not a single set of weights that make all of the rows’ predictors perfectly predict their targets with zero error with all of the same slope coefficients.

That’s why we can’t solve for slope coefficients like a normal system of equations! Like the title of this sub-section.

Recall that the original houses dataset ended up like this:

\[ \left[ \begin{array}{ccccc|c} 1450 & 3 & 2 & 1998 & 6000 & 320{,}000 \\ 2100 & 4 & 3 & 2012 & 8500 & 465{,}000 \\ 980 & 2 & 1 & 1965 & 3200 & 215{,}000 \end{array} \right] \]

With what we just learned, we know that there is no single solution that makes this system consistent. What we’re looking at is basically a non-square matrix, specifically the case where \(\#\text{ of constraints} > \#\text{ of unknowns}\), meaning there are no solutions to this.

We’d be in a similar situation if we had 10,000 observations

\[ \left[ \begin{array}{ccccc|c} 1450 & 3 & 2 & 1998 & 6000 & 320{,}000 \\ 2100 & 4 & 3 & 2012 & 8500 & 465{,}000 \\ 980 & 2 & 1 & 1965 & 3200 & 215{,}000 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \end{array} \right] \left.\vphantom{\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \end{array}}\right\} \ 10{,}000 \text{ equations} \]

There would only be a single solution forcing consistency for if we had exactly 5 (five) observations.

\[ \left[ \begin{array}{ccccc|c} 1450 & 3 & 2 & 1998 & 6000 & 320{,}000 \\ 2100 & 4 & 3 & 2012 & 8500 & 465{,}000 \\ 980 & 2 & 1 & 1965 & 3200 & 215{,}000 \\ 1720 & 3 & 2 & 2005 & 7100 & 385{,}000 \\ 1280 & 2 & 2 & 1979 & 4800 & 270{,}000 \end{array} \right] \]

Hm. I mean so we can’t rely on solving for the weights with consistency.

And if you used linear regression before, you know that the weights never force consistency! There’s always some level of error. Maybe you even know that we find the coefficients that minimize the aggregate error. MAYBE you even know that we call that method “Ordinary Least Squares” (OLS).

So yes. The rest of this essay is going to be how we use linear algebra to perform OLS.
