Preface

If you read the previous piece “Explaining Linear Algebra”, then you understand that math is just rules and things.

We’re about to talk about something called a “vector space”. And it’s going to be our basic set of rules and the things we can subject to those rules going forward.

1. Our baseline, a vector space

This is the definition of a vector space:

NoteDefinition: Vector space

A vector space \(V\) is defined as \(V = (\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{F}, \cdot, +)\) where:

  • \(V\) is a set of vectors
  • \(F\) is a field of scalars

And with two operations, \(\cdot\) and \(+\) defined as follows:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{vector addition:} && + : V \times V \to V, && (\vec{u}, \vec{v}) \mapsto \vec{u} + \vec{v} \\ &\text{scalar multiplication:} && \cdot : \mathbb{F} \times V \to V, && (a, \vec{v}) \mapsto a\vec{v} \end{aligned} \]

such that for all \(\vec{u}, \vec{v}, \vec{w} \in V\) and all \(a, b \in \mathbb{F}\), the following axioms hold:

\[ \begin{array}{rll} 1. & \vec{u} + \vec{v} = \vec{v} + \vec{u} & \text{(commutativity of addition)} \\ 2. & (\vec{u} + \vec{v}) + \vec{w} = \vec{u} + (\vec{v} + \vec{w}) & \text{(associativity of addition)} \\ 3. & \exists\, \vec{0} \in V \text{ such that } \vec{v} + \vec{0} = \vec{v} & \text{(additive identity)} \\ 4. & \forall\, \vec{v} \in V,\ \exists\, {-\vec{v}} \in V \text{ such that } \vec{v} + (-\vec{v}) = \vec{0} & \text{(additive inverse)} \\ 5. & a(b\vec{v}) = (ab)\vec{v} & \text{(compatibility of scalar multiplication)} \\ 6. & 1\vec{v} = \vec{v} & \text{(multiplicative identity)} \\ 7. & a(\vec{u} + \vec{v}) = a\vec{u} + a\vec{v} & \text{(distributivity over vector addition)} \\ 8. & (a + b)\vec{v} = a\vec{v} + b\vec{v} & \text{(distributivity over scalar addition)} \end{array} \]

I’ve made this definition collapsible so you can brush it out of your way. If you don’t have time to meticulously go over the definition of a vector space, this explanation should give you a totally fine understanding of what it is (at least for our purposes):

A vector space is a mathematical structure which creates criteria for a set of vectors \(V\).

Furthermore, objects which pass the criteria of a vector are valid under the two defined operations, \(+\) which we call “vector addition” and \(\cdot\) which we call “scalar multiplication”.

In the next section, we’re going to break down exactly what that means for us.

2.1 Introducing: Vectors and matrices

A vector is anything that passes that criteria. And we’re interested in those things, the objects that pass the criteria of a vector space.

For example. Does a duck qualify in the vector space? If it did, we’d be able to call it a “vector”. And if it’s a vector, we could perform scalar multiplication, vector addition, etc on it. The duck, that is.

So let’s check.

We say that “anything” that passes the criteria of a vector space qualifies as a vector. But in reality, you’ll find that the rules are so narrow, that things like a duck would never qualify. A log would never qualify. The qualify in the vector space, that “thing” is basically always a very precisely defined mathematical object. But if you found some kind of duck or trivial thing that qualified in the vector space, that would definitely be groundbreaking.

So now look at this, the vector \(\vec{x} = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)\).

Well let’s check all of the criteria.

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{u} + \vec{v} &= (u_1 + v_1,\ u_2 + v_2,\ \dots,\ u_5 + v_5) \\ a\vec{v} &= (a v_1,\ a v_2,\ \dots,\ a v_5) \\[6pt] \vec{y} = (5, 4, 3, 2, 1), \quad \vec{w} &= (0, 1, 0, 1, 0), \quad a = 2, \quad b = 3 \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{array}{rl} 1. & \vec{x} + \vec{y} = (6, 6, 6, 6, 6) = \vec{y} + \vec{x} \\ 2. & (\vec{x} + \vec{y}) + \vec{w} = (6, 7, 6, 7, 6) = \vec{x} + (\vec{y} + \vec{w}) \\ 3. & \vec{x} + (0, 0, 0, 0, 0) = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) = \vec{x} \\ 4. & \vec{x} + (-1, -2, -3, -4, -5) = (0, 0, 0, 0, 0) = \vec{0} \\ 5. & 2(3\vec{x}) = 2(3, 6, 9, 12, 15) = (6, 12, 18, 24, 30) = 6\vec{x} \\ 6. & 1\vec{x} = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) = \vec{x} \\ 7. & 2(\vec{x} + \vec{y}) = (12, 12, 12, 12, 12) = (2, 4, 6, 8, 10) + (10, 8, 6, 4, 2) = 2\vec{x} + 2\vec{y} \\ 8. & (2 + 3)\vec{x} = (5, 10, 15, 20, 25) = (2, 4, 6, 8, 10) + (3, 6, 9, 12, 15) = 2\vec{x} + 3\vec{x} \end{array} \]

This qualifies as a vector!

How about this?

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3} \]

This is a special case of a vector, where the entries are arranged in \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns, so it lives in \(\mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\). We call it a matrix.

Now let’s move on.

We have an idea of what types of things that qualify in our space (which pass our criteria). Now we’re going to move on and take a look at the operations that we define on them.

2.2 Introducing: Our two fundamental operations

If you read the earlier piece “Explaining Linear Algebra” then you know that math is always fundamentally things and rules. Objects and what we can do with them.

In our case here, establishing something called a “vector space” gave us our things, vectors and matrices.

We’re about to see what we can do with them.

2.2.1 Vector addition \((+)\)

\(+ : \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n\)

Vector addition is component-wise. The operation is only valid for vectors of the same length. You can’t add a vector \(\in \mathbb{R}^n\) to a vector \(\in \mathbb{R}^{n+1}\). That makes things easy for us. Under valid vector addition, this means all we have to do is line up the vectors component-to-component and add along the entire length of the vector.

Note: because there’s negatives, we don’t need to define a unique “vector subtraction”.

\[ \vec{u} + \vec{v} = (u_1 + v_1,\ u_2 + v_2,\ \dots,\ u_n + v_n) \]

For for vectors \(\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}\)

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + 4 \\ 2 + 5 \\ 3 + 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 7 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

And for vectors \(\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\)

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + (-3) \\ -1 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

2.2.2 Scalar multiplication \((\cdot)\)

\(\cdot : \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n\)

Scalar multiplication is component-wise. You multiply each of the vector’s components by the scalar, and that’s your answer! Effectively this is just stretching or squeezing the contents of the vector.

\[ a\vec{v} = (a v_1,\ a v_2,\ \dots,\ a v_n) \]

For scalar \(a = 3\) and vector \(\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\)

\[ 3 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \cdot 1 \\ 3 \cdot 2 \\ 3 \cdot 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

3. Additional operations

The definition of a vector space gives us these operations out of the box:

Vector operations:

  • Vector addition
  • Scalar multiplication

But basic linear algebra encompasses these addiitonal operations:

Vector operations:

  • Dot product

Matrix operations:

  • Matrix-vector products
  • Matrix-matrix products

There are three new operations that we haven’t seen yet. Here they are!

Vector operations: dot product

\(\cdot : \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\)

The dot product is component-wise. The operation is only valid for vectors of the same length. It’s simple! You just line up the two vectors alongside each other, and compute the components of your result one by one.

\[ \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + \dots + u_n v_n \]

For vectors \(\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}\)

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = (1)(4) + (2)(5) + (3)(6) = 4 + 10 + 18 = 32 \]

Matrix operations: matrix-vector product

\(A\vec{x} : \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\)

\[ A\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{a}_1 \cdot \vec{x} \\ \vdots \\ \vec{a}_m \cdot \vec{x} \end{bmatrix} = x_1 \vec{c}_1 + x_2 \vec{c}_2 + \dots + x_n \vec{c}_n \]

For matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\) and vector \(\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\), row by row

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (1)(1) + (2)(0) + (3)(-1) \\ (4)(1) + (5)(0) + (6)(-1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Matrix operations: matrix-matrix product

\(AB : \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \times \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} \to \mathbb{R}^{m \times p}\)

\[ (AB)_{ij} = \vec{a}_i \cdot \vec{b}_j = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj} \]

For matrices \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\)

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (1)(1) + (2)(0) + (3)(1) & (1)(0) + (2)(1) + (3)(1) \\ (4)(1) + (5)(0) + (6)(1) & (4)(0) + (5)(1) + (6)(1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 \\ 10 & 11 \end{bmatrix} \]
